**What was measured (DO y3, al-runner `edge-4ebfe7913`).**
- Test execution inside mutant requests is the largest share of the run: about 17,330 s of wall out of 28,222 s.
- **Survivors hold most of it:** of the 11,453 s of per-mutant time, survived mutants hold 9,829 s (86%), because a
  survivor runs every covering test. Mutants with more than 100 covering tests (5%) hold 46%.
- **Half of those tests never reach the mutated statement.** Over 8,965 survivors with reach facts, `reachedBy` (the tests
  whose run reached the mutant's guard) is 67,582 of 130,979 covering tests (52%), and 2,835 survivors were reached by no
  covering test at all.

**Why pruning is safe in principle.** A deterministic test that does not reach the mutated statement runs exactly as it
does unmutated. The one exception is state left by an EARLIER test of the same codeunit that did reach it (a codeunit's
tests share state under the codeunit isolation). So the safe unit is per codeunit:
- keep every test of a codeunit up to and including its last reaching test;
- drop a codeunit with no reaching test.
- Reach would come from a baseline run with the reach latches recorded per test (statement grain), not from the mutant
  runs.

**What would decide it:**
1. Offline from y3's per-test rows: the seconds survivors spent in tests the codeunit-order rule would drop. This is the
   upper bound of the saving.
2. Where per-test reach comes from at baseline (does v3's baseline record the latches per test today?), and what it costs.
3. A v3 A/B on a DO slice, pruned against unpruned, compared per mutant: **no verdict, killing test or covering set may
   move**, except covering sets shrinking to the reaching tests. Then a spec-light for a ruling.
